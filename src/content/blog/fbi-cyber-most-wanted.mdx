The FBI has a "Ten Most Wanted Fugitives" list that most people know from movies. Fewer people know it also keeps a list just for hackers: **Cyber's Most Wanted**. Reading it is like reading the credits of every big cybercrime story of the last fifteen years.

I go through it now and then, not because I expect to meet anyone on it, but because every name is a lesson in how real attacks are built.

## The banking-malware godfather

**Evgeniy Bogachev** is probably the most famous face on the list. US authorities say he ran **GameOver Zeus**, a network of infected computers used to steal banking credentials, and helped spread **CryptoLocker**, one of the early ransomware hits. Losses were estimated at more than $100 million.

The US offered up to **$3 million** for information leading to his arrest, at the time one of the biggest rewards ever for a cyber suspect. He is believed to live in Russia, and he has never been arrested.

## The guy with the "thief" plate

**Maksim Yakubets** is accused of leading **Evil Corp**, the group behind the **Dridex** banking malware. When the US charged him in 2019, it offered up to **$5 million** for information about him.

What made the story famous were the videos: a customised Lamborghini, reportedly with a number plate reading "thief" in Russian. Cybercrime as a lifestyle brand. The charges and sanctions made that lifestyle a lot harder to spend.

## The state hacker

**Park Jin Hyok** is different. He is not a freelancer. The US Justice Department charged him in 2018 as a programmer working for a North Korean government front company. The indictment ties him to the **Sony Pictures** hack, the [Bangladesh Bank heist](/blog/bangladesh-bank-heist-typo/) and [WannaCry](/blog/wannacry-kill-switch/). In other words, to [Lazarus Group](/blog/lazarus-group-hackers-who-fund-a-nation/).

He will almost certainly never stand in a US courtroom. So why bother charging him?

## The ransomware bosses

The modern list is full of ransomware names. **Mikhail Matveev** was charged in 2023 over attacks using LockBit, Babuk and Hive, with a reward of up to **$10 million**. **Dmitry Khoroshev**, named as the leader of LockBit in 2024, carries the same reward. I covered how police got inside his operation in [Operation Cronos](/blog/lockbit-operation-cronos/).

## Why the list works anyway

It is easy to laugh at a "most wanted" list where most people live out of reach. But I think it works, in three quiet ways.

**Travel becomes a gamble.** An indictment follows you forever. **Roman Seleznev**, a Russian carder, was arrested in the **Maldives** in 2014 while on holiday, flown to the US and later sentenced to **27 years**. Every beach trip is now a risk calculation.

**Money gets frozen.** Charges often come with sanctions. Exchanges, banks and partners stop touching your wallets. Stolen money is only useful if you can spend it.

**Trust breaks.** Cybercrime runs on reputation. Once a leader is unmasked, affiliates start to wonder what else the police know, and who is talking.

## What I take from it as a builder

None of these people broke into systems with movie magic. Banking trojans stole saved passwords. Ransomware crews bought access from other criminals. A state team sent convincing emails. The defences are still the unglamorous ones: MFA everywhere, patched systems, least privilege and backups you have actually tested.

## The honest summary

The Cyber's Most Wanted list is less a list of geniuses and more a list of business models. Each name represents a way of turning weak security into money. Close those gaps, and you are simply not worth their time.
