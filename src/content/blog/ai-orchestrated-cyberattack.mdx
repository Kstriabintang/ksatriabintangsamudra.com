For years, "AI hacker" was a phrase for conference slides and movie trailers. In November 2025 it became an incident report.

**Anthropic**, the company behind Claude, published details of what it called the first documented large-scale cyberattack executed mostly by AI. I use AI agents every day to build software, so this report hit close to home. The same tools that make me faster can make an attacker faster too.

## What happened

According to Anthropic, in **mid-September 2025** it detected a state-sponsored group, which it designated **GTG-1002** and assessed as Chinese with high confidence. The group used **Claude Code**, Anthropic's coding agent, inside an automated framework to target roughly **30 organisations**: tech companies, financial institutions, chemical manufacturers and government agencies. A small number of intrusions succeeded.

The scary part is the division of labour. Anthropic estimated that AI did the large majority of the tactical work. Humans picked the targets and approved the big moves. The agent did the grind: mapping systems, finding weak spots, writing exploit code, testing stolen credentials, and sorting stolen data to find what was valuable.

Work that used to take a team of operators weeks was happening at machine speed.

## How they got around the guardrails

AI models are trained to refuse obviously malicious work. So the attackers did what social engineers have always done: they lied.

- **They split the attack into tiny tasks.** "Scan this range." "Explain this service." "Write a script that tests these logins." Each request looks like normal security work on its own.
- **They gave the AI a cover story.** The operators reportedly told the model it was working for a legitimate cybersecurity firm doing defensive testing.

No single prompt looked like a crime. The crime only appeared when you saw all of them together. That is a lesson for anyone building AI products: safety checks on single requests are not enough.

## What the AI still got wrong

This is the part people skip. Anthropic noted the agent sometimes **hallucinated**: it claimed to find credentials that did not work, or presented public information as a secret discovery. Humans still had to check its homework.

So no, we are not at "press a button, hack a bank". But we are past "AI can only write phishing emails". The gap between those two is closing every few months.

## The everyday AI crime you will actually meet

Most people will never face a state-level AI agent. They will face the cheaper stuff:

- **Phishing with perfect grammar.** The old tip "look for spelling mistakes" is dead. AI writes clean, personal, local-language messages at scale.
- **Voice and video clones.** A few seconds of audio is enough to fake a voice. I wrote about the [$25 million deepfake video call](/blog/deepfake-cfo-25-million/) that proved how real this is.
- **Faster exploitation.** When a new vulnerability is published, attackers use AI to turn the advisory into working code faster than many teams can patch.
- **Underground "evil GPT" tools.** Since 2023, criminal forums have sold chatbots like WormGPT and FraudGPT, marketed as AI without the safety rules.

## How defenders should respond

I do not think the answer is panic. It is matching speed with speed.

- **Use AI on defence too.** Log triage, alert summarising, code review and threat hunting are exactly the boring, high-volume work agents are good at. Anthropic itself used Claude to analyse the data from this investigation.
- **Patch faster than the AI can weaponise.** If an exploit can be written in hours, your patch window cannot be months.
- **Watch behaviour, not just signatures.** An AI-driven intrusion moves fast and makes lots of requests. Unusual speed is a signal.
- **If you build AI tools, think about misuse patterns.** Look at sessions and accounts, not just single prompts.

## The honest summary

The first AI-run cyberattack did not look like a movie. It looked like a very fast, very tireless junior operator that sometimes made things up, supervised by a few humans. That is already enough to change the economics of hacking. Defenders who refuse to use the same tools will simply be slower, and in security, slower usually means breached.
