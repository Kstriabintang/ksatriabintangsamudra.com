For years the advice for spotting a scam was simple: if you are not sure, get them on a video call. If you can see and hear the person, it is probably real.

In 2024 that advice died in a meeting room in Hong Kong.

## What happened

In **January 2024**, a finance employee at the Hong Kong office of **Arup**, the British engineering firm behind landmarks like the Sydney Opera House, received a message that appeared to come from the company's **UK-based chief financial officer**. It talked about a confidential transaction.

The employee was suspicious. Good instinct. So they joined a **video call** to check.

On the call: the CFO, plus several colleagues they recognised. Faces, voices, mannerisms. Everything looked right. The instructions were clear, so the employee followed them.

Over the following days they made **15 transfers** to **five bank accounts**, totalling **HK$200 million**, around **US$25 million**.

Every other person on that call was an **AI-generated deepfake**. The fraud was only discovered when the employee later checked with head office. Hong Kong police said it was the first time they had seen a fake multi-person video conference used this way, and Arup confirmed it was the victim in May 2024.

## Why it worked

What I find brilliant, in a terrible way, is that the attackers did not hack Arup's systems at all. No malware. No stolen passwords. Arup itself later said its systems were not compromised. They hacked **trust**.

- **Authority.** The request came from the CFO. Most employees do not argue with the CFO.
- **Social proof.** It was not one fake person, it was a whole meeting. If everyone else on the call acts normal, you assume it is normal.
- **Secrecy and urgency.** A "confidential" deal gives a reason not to ask around.
- **Public raw material.** Executives appear in videos, interviews and conference talks. That footage is the training data for their clone.

This is classic [social engineering](/blog/ai-orchestrated-cyberattack/), just upgraded with AI. The playbook is decades old. The special effects are new.

## It is not the first, and it will not be the last

Back in 2019, the Wall Street Journal reported a UK energy company being tricked into sending around €220,000 after a call where AI imitated the voice of its parent company's boss. That was voice only. Five years later it was a full video meeting.

Today, voice-cloning tools need only a short sample. Real-time face-swap software runs on a gaming laptop. The cost of faking a person has collapsed.

## How I would defend against this

Here is my honest take: **do not rely on spotting the fake**. Detection tools help, but the tells keep disappearing. Design the process so that even a perfect fake cannot move money.

- **Out-of-band verification.** If someone asks for money on a call or chat, hang up and call them back on a number you already had. Not the one in the message.
- **Two-person rule.** No single employee should be able to send a large or unusual payment alone, no matter who asked.
- **Verification phrases.** Some teams and families now agree on a private code word for emergencies. Low tech, very effective.
- **Treat secrecy plus urgency as a red flag.** Real CFOs can wait ten minutes for a callback.
- **Limit the public footage of key people** where it makes sense, and teach executives that their voice is now a security asset.

## The honest summary

The Arup scam is the moment "I saw them with my own eyes" stopped being proof. The attackers did not need to break any system, because the human approval process was the system. Make that process require more than one channel and more than one person, and a deepfake becomes just an expensive video.
