On **Friday, 12 May 2017**, hospital staff across England walked up to their computers and found a red window demanding Bitcoin. Ambulances were diverted. Appointments were cancelled. At the same time, factories, telecoms and train stations around the world were seeing the same screen.

The ransomware was called **WannaCry**. And the craziest part of the story is how it ended: a young researcher spent about **$10.69** on a domain name, and the spread stopped.

## Why it spread so fast

Most ransomware needs someone to click something. WannaCry did not. It was a **worm**: once on a machine, it scanned for other Windows computers and infected them on its own.

The engine was an exploit called **EternalBlue**, targeting a flaw in SMBv1, the old Windows file-sharing protocol. EternalBlue had been developed by the US National Security Agency and leaked online by a group called the Shadow Brokers in April 2017.

Here is the detail that hurts: Microsoft had already released a patch for that flaw, **MS17-010**, in **March 2017**. Two months before WannaCry. The machines that went down were mostly the ones that had not installed it, or were too old to receive it.

## The damage

Estimates put infections at **hundreds of thousands of computers in more than 150 countries**. The UK's National Health Service was the most visible victim, with thousands of appointments and operations cancelled. Car plants paused production. Payment demands started at $300 in Bitcoin and doubled if you waited.

The ransom haul itself was actually small. The real cost was the downtime, billions according to many estimates, because a worm does not care whether you are a hospital or a coffee shop.

## The $10.69 kill switch

**Marcus Hutchins**, a 22-year-old British researcher known online as MalwareTech, was looking at a sample of the malware. He noticed it tried to connect to a long, nonsense domain name before doing anything else. That domain was **not registered**.

So he registered it, for around $10.69, mostly to track infections.

It turned out the malware's logic was roughly: "if this domain answers, stop". Once his domain went live, new infections stopped encrypting. One of the most damaging cyberattacks in history slowed to a halt because of a cheap domain and a curious person.

Why would malware have such a switch? The leading theory is that it was meant to detect security sandboxes, which often answer every domain request. Whatever the intent, it saved a lot of systems that weekend.

In December 2017, the US and UK publicly attributed WannaCry to **North Korea**, the same [Lazarus Group](/blog/lazarus-group-hackers-who-fund-a-nation/) behind other headline attacks.

## What WannaCry teaches every engineer

I keep these four lessons in my head whenever I set up infrastructure:

- **Patch like your business depends on it.** A two-month-old patch would have stopped WannaCry cold. "We will update after the release" is how incidents start.
- **Kill legacy protocols.** SMBv1 was obsolete for years. If you do not need an old service, turn it off. Every unused service is an unlocked door.
- **Segment your network.** A worm spreads sideways. If one infected laptop can reach every server, the whole organisation is one machine wide.
- **Back up, offline, and test the restore.** Ransomware is only scary when you cannot rebuild without paying.

## The honest summary

WannaCry was not stopped by an expensive security product. It was stopped by one person reading the code carefully, and it could have been prevented by a patch that already existed. That combination of boring discipline and curious humans is still the best defence we have.
